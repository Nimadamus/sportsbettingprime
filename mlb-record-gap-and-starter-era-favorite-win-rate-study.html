In 2026 A .100 To .199 Record Edge Won 50.6 Percent Of The Time Without An Ace On The Mound And 68.5 With One

Here's a number that should bother anyone who prices a moneyline off the standings. In 2026, when one team's winning percentage was between .100 and .199 better than its opponent's going into the game, the better team won 54.2 percent of the time. That's 293 wins in 541 games. A .100 gap is roughly the difference between a 90 win team and a 75 win team, and it bought about four points over a coin flip.

Four points doesn't pay -162. It doesn't pay -124 either. So the question for Wednesday's prices isn't whether the favorite is the better team. It's what, beyond the standings, turns a better team into one that actually wins often enough. The answer in this season's data is the starting pitcher, and the effect is large enough that the record gap almost disappears next to it.

What we measured

1,963 completed regular season games from April 18 through September 15, 2026. Every game from Opening Day on was replayed in order, and each team's winning percentage was taken as it stood on the morning of the game, so no game sees its own result or anything after it. A game only counts once both teams had played at least 20, which is why the window opens on April 18. Two games without a listed starter were dropped.

The starter for each side comes from the MLB Stats API schedule record for that game. A starter counts as an ace here when his 2026 season ERA is under 3.00 with at least 50 innings and 8 starts. Everyone else, including good pitchers who haven't thrown enough yet, is in the other group.

Read the second pair of rows slowly. A road team with a .100 to .199 record edge and an ordinary starter won 46.1 percent of 230 games. It lost more than it won. Put a pitcher with an ERA under 3.00 on the mound for that same kind of road team and it won 65.6 percent. The starter is worth nearly 20 points. The record gap, holding the starter fixed, is worth one or two.

The bottom two rows are small, 16 and 18 games, so we wouldn't lean on them. The top four rows have enough games to take seriously.

Measured at the three moneylines

Brewers ML at -124. Milwaukee is .623 and Pittsburgh is .497, a gap of .126, and the Brewers are on the road. Logan Henderson has a 2.34 ERA over 88.1 innings and 16 starts, so he qualifies. That bucket, road teams with a .100 to .199 edge and a sub 3.00 starter, won 65.6 percent of 61 games against a break even of 55.36. This is the cleanest fit on the card. It's also a reminder of how much the price depends on who's pitching: the same Brewers with an ordinary starter would sit in the 46.1 percent row.

Rays ML at -173. Tampa Bay is .607 and the Athletics are .404, a gap of .203, at home. Nick Martinez has a 2.92 ERA in 166.1 innings. The exact bucket is only 16 games at 68.8 percent, too thin to carry a number. Widen it to home teams with a gap of .150 or more and an ace on the mound and it's 25 wins in 37 games, 67.6 percent, about four points clear of 63.37. That's a pass on base rates, with the caveat that 37 games is not many. One more wrinkle belongs here: Martinez has a 3.92 ERA in 14 home starts and 1.99 in 14 road starts, so the season number flatters what he's done in this particular building.

Padres ML at -162. San Diego is .543 and Colorado is .371, a gap of .172, on the road. Robbie Ray's ERA is 3.57, so he's in the other group, and road teams in that bucket won 46.1 percent of 230 games. On the pitcher model this ticket is nowhere near 61.83. The one cut that rescues it is the park. Road teams with a record edge of .150 or more at Coors Field won 16 of 21, 76.2 percent, and every road team at Coors in the study window won 39 of 65, 60.0 percent. Twenty one games is a small sample, so the honest summary is that the Padres price is a bet on Colorado at altitude, not on the starter.

Measured at the two unders

Athletics team total under 3.5 at -131. Across the study window, road teams scored three runs or fewer 46.1 percent of the time. Against a home starter with an ERA under 3.00 it was 163 of 256, 63.7 percent. When that home team also had a record edge of .150 or more, it was 24 of 37, 64.9 percent. Both clear the 56.71 break even by around seven points. The A's on the road this season have been held to three or fewer in 40 of 76 games, 52.6 percent, and their .658 road OPS is where that comes from. The same home and road split on Martinez applies here too.

Dodgers and Reds under 8.5 at -120. This is the ticket the study likes least. All games finished with 8 runs or fewer 50.9 percent of the time. With the road team holding a .100 to .199 record edge, it was 52.2 percent of 293 games. Take every road team with an edge of .100 or more whose starter wasn't in the sub 3.00 group and it fell to 47.9 percent of 288. The Dodgers' edge tonight is .146. Blake Snell is in that group only because of the innings floor: his ERA is 1.91, but it's over 37.2 innings and 7 starts. So the study can't see the reason for this bet. Andrew Abbott, on the other side, has a 4.78 ERA in 30 starts. If you're on this under, you're betting that Snell's seven starts are real, and you should know the base rate doesn't back you.

Three of the five clear their break even on base rates. The two that don't are the two where the card is leaning on something the buckets can't measure: altitude at Coors for the Padres and a short, very good Snell sample for the under.

What this study does not tell you

The records are clean, taken the morning of each game. The ERA groups aren't: they use each pitcher's season ERA as of Wednesday, which includes the games being measured, so the sub 3.00 group knows a little more than anyone knew in May. Pitchers traded midseason, like Ray, carry one combined ERA. The study doesn't see bullpens, lineups, weather or rest. And a moneyline bucket measures how often a team won, not whether the price was fair on the day. Base rates tell you which tickets need a reason. They don't supply the reason.
