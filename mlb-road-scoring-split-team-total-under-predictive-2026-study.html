In 2026 A Team Scoring Half A Run Less On The Road Scored 4.50 Per Road Game, Right At Its Full Season Average

Every team total preview reaches for the same split. This lineup scores 3.59 runs a game on the road, so the road under writes itself. It sounds like the most specific number you could use. So we went back through the 2026 season and tested it the plain way: before every road game, what did the team's road average say it would score, what did its full season average say, and what did it actually score?

The road split lost. Teams scoring half a run or more less on the road than their season average were supposed to score 3.79 by that split. They scored 4.50, almost exactly their full season number of 4.45. Teams that looked half a run better on the road were supposed to score 4.95 and scored 4.53. Over 1,695 road games, the full season average missed the actual score by 2.576 runs on average and the road average missed by 2.588. The more specific number was slightly worse.

What we measured

1,695 road team games from May 5 through September 17, 2026, every road game in which the visiting team had already played at least 20 road games. All 2,295 final scores from Opening Day on were replayed in date order, so each team's road average, its full season average and its opponent's runs allowed are exactly what they were before first pitch. Runs are final runs, extra innings included.

Read the last column top to bottom and it barely moves, 4.40 to 4.57. Whatever made a team look bad on the road through the first few months didn't follow it into the next road game. Most of what looks like a road split, 30 or 50 games in, looks like the schedule it happened to draw and plain noise.

Held to three or fewer

A team total under 3.5 doesn't care about averages; it cares how often a team gets stuck at three. Across the study the road team was held to three or fewer 774 times in 1,695, 45.7 percent. Split by the road average going in:

Royals under 3.5 at -125. This is the ticket the study was built for. Kansas City scores 3.59 a game on the road and 4.20 overall, a gap of 0.61, and it's been held to three or fewer in 48 of 78 road games. That's exactly the profile the table says to discount: teams 0.50 or more worse on the road scored 4.50 their next time out and stayed at three or fewer 47.7 percent of the time. Pittsburgh allows 4.61 runs a game, which puts this game in a bucket where visitors were held to three or fewer 46.5 percent of the time. So the split can't carry this bet. Paul Skenes has to, and he might: a 3.78 ERA and 185 strikeouts in 162 innings. The catch is that Pittsburgh's opponents scored three or fewer in only 11 of his 30 starts, because the Pirates' bullpen finishes his games. On our numbers the Royals under needs Skenes to go deep.

Braves under 4.5 at -139. Atlanta's road gap is smaller, 4.37 on the road against 4.60 overall, and teams in that range stayed at four or fewer 55.8 percent of the time. Houston allows 4.78 a game, a group where visitors stayed at four or fewer 59.0 percent. That's already right at the 58.16 percent break even before you look at the pitcher. Peter Lambert, with a 3.71 ERA and a .649 OPS against, has held opponents to four or fewer in 17 of his 26 starts. Of the two unders, this is the one the numbers stand behind.

The four sides. They aren't team total bets, but the same lesson applies: the pitcher and the opponent tell you more than a split. Guardians -1 rests on Mason Barnett's 7.08 ERA, and on a Cleveland team that has won by two or more in 24 of 78 home games. Blue Jays ML rests on Dylan Cease's 2.38 ERA, 1.89 in 13 road starts. Yankees ML at -110 is a bet on an 88 and 64 team with a +136 run differential against Eduardo Rodriguez's 2.62 ERA. Mariners ML at -181 rests on Colorado being 56 and 97, and on Bryan Woo, whose 5.23 road ERA is the split to watch. This study measured offenses, not pitchers, so read that pitching split with the same caution rather than as proof in either direction.
