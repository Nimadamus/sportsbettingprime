The Public Took The Under In All Eight Games That Night

Historical · not a current board

This was the board as of the date shown. Any price shown below is the price that was available at that time, not a current quote. The original analysis is unchanged; present-tense wording has been corrected so nothing reads as live.

Eight games are on that night's MLB board, and the under is the majority ticket side in every single one of them. Not six. Not seven. Eight for eight, ranging from a 58 percent lean in the Bronx to a 72 percent pile-on in Houston. A run that clean is worth stopping on by itself, because the public almost never agrees with itself that thoroughly across a full slate. What makes it interesting is the second column. In seven of those eight games, the money percentage sits further toward the over than the ticket percentage does, and in four of them the money is on the over outright while the tickets are on the under.

That gap is the whole story of this Monday. Small bets are buying unders. Larger bets, in aggregate, are not.

Both prices in that table are the numbers actually taken, at a book pricing each game shorter than the retail screen was offering this afternoon.

Now the part that cuts against the card, because it does cut. New York at minus 192 is the most crowded position on this entire slate. Eighty-seven percent of tickets and 92 percent of the money are already on the Yankees to win the game, which means there is no contrarian argument available and no point pretending one exists. Backing a side that heavy is backing it after the market has finished pricing it, and the only thing recovered is a shorter number than the screen. Milwaukee has a different problem, and it is standing on the mound. Brandon Sproat carries a 5.05 ERA, 4.47 walks per nine and 1.65 home runs allowed per nine across 92.2 innings, the worst starting line on either side of this card, and 71 percent of the money in that game is on the over of 8.5, which is a direct vote against him.

What gets bought in both cases is the club, not the matchup. Milwaukee is 69-42, the best record in baseball, and 36-20 at American Family Field. New York is 63-49 with a plus 87 run differential, facing a St. Louis team at 55-57 that has gone 3-7 in its last ten. Pittsburgh arrives 56-57 and also 3-7 across its last ten. Those are four-month arguments about rosters, not arguments about who happens to be starting that night, and the rest of this page is a market read that does not line up neatly behind either of them.

Yankee Stadium: The Widest Gap On The Board Sits Behind The Best Arm On The Board

St. Louis at New York carries a total of 8, and 58 percent of tickets want it lower. The reasoning writes itself. Cam Schlittler is 10-6 with a 2.04 ERA, a 0.91 WHIP, and 165 strikeouts against 26 walks in 136.2 innings, and opponents are hitting .196 off him. The Yankee bullpen behind him owns a 3.28 ERA. Michael McGreevy counters at 3.57 for a Cardinals club that has lost seven of ten. On paper this is the most obvious under on the card.

The money went 76 percent to the over. That is a 34-point separation from the ticket count and the largest gap of the night, across 2,539 logged bets, which is the second-heaviest game on the board. Somebody is looking at a total of 8 in a ballpark where the Yankees have hit 157 home runs while batting .232 as a team, and deciding the number has been shaded too far by the pitcher's name.

Cam Schlittler: 2.04 ERA, 0.91 WHIP, .196 opponent average. The total behind him is 8, and the handle is on the over.

Alongside that total sits the moneyline, minus 200 on the screen with 87 percent of tickets and 92 percent of the money. Nobody is fading New York to win. The disagreement is strictly about how many runs get scored, which is a cleaner signal than a split where the public and the handle are fighting over the side.

That is also the exact square the card stands on. Two units on the Yankees at minus 192 buys the least controversial idea anyone holds that night, at a shorter number than the board was showing, and it sits directly next to a handle that put 76 percent of its dollars on the game going over 8. Both of those can land together. New York wins 63-49 baseball against a 55-57 club that has dropped seven of ten, and Schlittler still fails to hold a total of 8 down. The play only needs the first half of that sentence, which is the whole reason it is on the side and not on the number.

Milwaukee: A 69-42 Team, Two Starters Who Cannot Find The Zone

Pittsburgh at Milwaukee runs the same pattern at 8.5, with 62 percent of tickets on the under and 71 percent of the money on the over, a 33-point gap. Look at the two starters and the handle's position gets easier to understand. Bubba Chandler is 4.56 with a 4.90 walks-per-nine rate. Brandon Sproat is 5.05 with a 4.47 walks-per-nine rate and 1.65 home runs allowed per nine across 92.2 innings. Two young arms, both with a base-on-balls problem, in a game the market has priced at 8.5.

Milwaukee is 69-42, the best record in the game, and its plus 135 run differential is the second best in baseball behind the Dodgers at plus 150. Its bullpen has a 3.48 ERA, which is the argument for the under and clearly what the ticket majority is buying. The counterargument is that a strong bullpen only matters after the starters get through their innings, and neither man on the mound that night has shown he can do that without traffic.

This is also the game where the card and the market read pull against each other hardest. A unit and a half on the Brewers moneyline at minus 139 is a bet on the 69-42 club and its 36-20 home record, not on Sproat, and the 71 percent of dollars sitting on the over of 8.5 is the market saying out loud that Sproat is the vulnerability. Pittsburgh is not the pushover its record suggests either. The Pirates are 56-57 with a plus 30 run differential, better than St. Louis at minus 16, and Chandler has held opponents to a .229 average with 8.00 strikeouts per nine across 104.2 innings, a sharper line than the man opposite him. Laying that price means paying for the roster and swallowing the worse starter.

The public is buying reputation on the mound. The handle is buying walk rates. In four of the eight games that night, those two things point in opposite directions.
Houston: The Most Lopsided Ticket Count Of The Night, And The Money Will Not Follow

Seventy-two percent of tickets on Toronto at Houston want the under 9. That is the heaviest public lean on this slate. The money is 46 percent over, 54 percent under, which is close to a coin flip and a full 18 points away from where the tickets sit.

The starters explain the resistance. Shane Bieber has made seven starts with a 5.74 ERA and is walking 5.17 men per nine innings. Cristian Javier has made four with a 7.17 ERA and is walking 6.33 per nine. Two former front-line arms, both mid-rebuild, both currently unable to command the zone. Behind Javier sits a Houston bullpen issuing 4.31 walks per nine, the highest relief walk rate of any club on that night's board. Houston has won six straight and is 9-1 in its last ten, which is exactly the kind of streak that pulls in recreational tickets on both the side and the under, and the Astros are minus 135 with 79 percent of tickets and 91 percent of the money behind them. The run differential underneath that streak is minus 27.

Coors Field Is The One Place Everybody Agrees

Tampa Bay at Colorado is the single exception on the board. The total is 11.5, the under holds 62 percent of tickets, and the money is 61 percent under as well. A one-point gap. No disagreement at all.

That consensus is a little surprising given the pitching matchup. Michael Lorenzen takes the ball for Colorado at 3-9 with a 6.54 ERA, a 1.81 WHIP, and a .331 opponent batting average, which is the worst mark by a wide margin among the sixteen probable starters here. The Rockies have allowed 636 runs, roughly 180 more than the Rays, and carry a 5.46 team ERA. Ian Seymour answers for Tampa Bay with a 4.37 ERA, 90 strikeouts in 78.1 innings, and a .211 opponent average.

Michael Lorenzen: 6.54 ERA and a .331 opponent average, and the market still moved the number to 11.5 and stopped.

The read here is that 11.5 has already done the work. The market priced Coors and priced Lorenzen, and once a number climbs that far above the rest of the board, both the public and the handle stop treating the over as free money. That is what an efficiently shaded total looks like, and it is a useful contrast with the number 8 sitting in the Bronx.

One Side Worth Flagging: Texas

Giants at Rangers is the only pick'em on the board, both sides at minus 110. San Francisco holds 44 percent of tickets and 62 percent of the money. Texas holds 56 percent of tickets and 38 percent of the money. The handle is on the 47-65 team.

What makes that strange is the road split. San Francisco is 21-38 away from Oracle Park, the worst road record of any club playing that night. Logan Webb is the reason to look anyway, at 3.93 with a 1.12 WHIP and only nine home runs allowed in 119 innings, and Texas had not named a starter when this board was pulled. An unannounced arm against a known quantity is a common cause of exactly this kind of handle imbalance, and it is also the lightest game on the slate at 1,176 logged bets, which makes the percentage easier to move.

The Case Against Reading Too Much Into This

Ticket-versus-money divergence is a real signal and a noisy one, and it is worth being blunt about the limits. A money percentage is a share of dollars, not a measure of who is right. On a Monday in early August, with logged bet counts running between 1,176 and 2,600 per game rather than weekend volume, a handful of large wagers can swing a money share by twenty points without any special information behind them. The Texas game is the clearest candidate for that distortion.

The public lean is also not irrational here. Seven of the eight totals sit at 9.5 or lower, which reflects a board full of pitching matchups, and unders have a structural pull on recreational tickets because they are the cheaper-feeling side of a low number. A slate where the public takes eight straight unders is a slate where the public is broadly reading the same pitching matchups everyone else is. The question is not whether the under lean is defensible. It is whether the number has already been shaded far enough to pay for it, and on the Yankees and Brewers games the handle is voting that it has.

So the page closes where it opened, with the disagreement left standing. Three and a half units are down on two favorites, 1.5 on Milwaukee and 2 on New York, and both are bets on rosters that have been better for four months than the ones across from them. Everything else on this page argues with that in some way. The handle expects more runs than either total implies, and on the Yankees the side has already been picked clean by the whole market before a single dollar of this card went down. Neither fact moved the plays and neither has been hidden. Prices, totals and both percentage columns move by the hour, so check the number in front of you before it turns into a different bet than the one written here.
