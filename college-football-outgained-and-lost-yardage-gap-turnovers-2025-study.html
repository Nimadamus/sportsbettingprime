Outgaining Your Opponent By Under 50 Yards Is A Coin Flip, And It Gets Quoted Like Evidence

It is the most reliable line in a Monday column. The score said one thing, the box score said another, and the team that lost actually outgained the team that won. Move on from the scoreboard, the argument goes, because the yardage knows better.

Sometimes it does. We measured how often.

1,340 completed 2025 FBS games with a full team box score on both sides. For each one we took total yards, first downs, turnovers and the final score, and asked a single question: did the team that gained more yards win?

One game in five goes the other way

Across the whole population the answer is usually yes, and the exception is not rare.

The losing team outgained the winning team in 286 of 1,340 games, which is 21.34 percent. Slightly more than one game in five.

That is frequent enough that finding one is not a discovery. Any given Saturday produces a handful of them, and picking the one that suits an argument is not analysis.

The size of the edge is the whole thing

Here is where the Monday column breaks, because "outgained" is reported as a binary and it is nothing of the kind.

A yardage edge under 50 yards came with a win 54.87 percent of the time. Across the same 1,340 games, the home team won 63.21 percent. Which is to say that knowing nothing except who was at home was worth more than eight points of extra information over knowing who gained 40 more yards.

At 150 yards or more it is 96.16 percent and effectively settled. The useful range is everything below that, and the bottom bucket is noise.

What actually beat the yardage

Turnovers, and it is not close.

In the 286 games where the loser outgained the winner, the loser gave the ball away 1.97 times against the winner's 0.72. Everywhere else that split is 1.62 to 0.93, which is a far smaller gap.

Put directly: 197 of the 286 outgained-and-lost games, which is 68.88 percent, came with the outgaining team also losing the turnover battle.

So the sentence "they outgained them and lost" is, most of the time, a longer way of saying "they turned it over." The yardage is not the hidden truth underneath the result. The giveaways are the visible reason for it, sitting in the same box score.

The bucket that gets quoted is the bucket that means least

The 1 to 49 yard bucket is 339 games, the second largest on the page, and it is the one a writer is most likely to reach for, because a 40 yard edge is exactly the kind of margin that shows up in a game that felt close.

It resolved at 54.87 percent. Against a bare coin it is worth under five points. Against knowing home field it is worth less than nothing.

And in that bucket, when the outgaining team lost, its turnover margin was only +0.98, the smallest of any bucket. In other words the small yardage edge games that went the other way did not even need a turnover catastrophe to explain them. One giveaway and a short field did it.

Why the yardage overstates itself

Two mechanical reasons, neither exotic.

Trailing teams accumulate yards. A team down 17 in the fourth throws it on every down against a defense playing 12 yards off. Those drives produce yardage whether or not they produce points, and they land in the same total the column quotes on Monday.

A turnover deletes a drive and shortens the other one. An interception at midfield removes whatever yardage that possession would have generated and hands the opponent a scoring chance that requires almost none. The yardage column punishes the victim twice.

Total yards is a volume statistic being read as an efficiency statistic, and those come apart most violently in exactly the games people want to reinterpret.

What to do with it

One. Ask for the number before accepting the claim. "They outgained them" is not information until somebody says by how much. Under 50, discard it.

Two. Read the turnover line in the same breath. Seven times out of ten it is the explanation, and it is printed two rows down.

Three. A 150 yard edge that lost is worth looking at. Those are 495 games resolving at 96.16 percent, so the exceptions are genuinely strange and genuinely worth a minute.

Four. Home field is the baseline to beat. Any box score signal that resolves below 63.21 percent is not adding to what the venue already told you.

Method: 1,340 completed 2025 FBS regular season games pulled from the ESPN public college football API, restricted to games where both teams had a complete team box score and where neither the score nor the total yards were tied. Games ESPN did not publish full team statistics for are absent, so this is a large sample of the 2025 season rather than the whole of it. Total yards, first downs and turnovers are as published. Neutral site games are excluded from the home field figure because they have no home team. No betting lines are used anywhere on this page and nothing here is against the spread.
